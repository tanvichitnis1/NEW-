# EOV — Integrated Marketing Agency Sector
## Research Design & Protocol (Stage 1: Analyse)

**Status:** Draft for approval. No findings yet. This document defines *what will be
researched, how, from which sources, and to what evidence standard* — before any
analysis or deck work begins.

**Method context:** This is the **Analyse** stage of Analyse → Direction → Plan → Control.
Stages 2–4 (positioning, USP, the deck itself) are explicitly out of scope until this
stage is signed off.

## 1. Research objectives — the seven questions this must answer

Every workstream below traces to one of these. If a line of enquiry doesn't serve one,
it gets cut.

1. **Definition** — What exactly is the "integrated marketing agency services" market,
   where are its boundaries, and which part of it is EOV actually entering?
2. **Size & depth** — How large is the addressable pool in EOV's geography, how fast is
   it growing, and how *deep* is it (how many buyers, at what budget, how often, for how long)?
3. **Structure & economics** — How does this industry make money, and what does a
   viable P&L look like at EOV's scale?
4. **Competition** — Who are the top 15 agencies EOV must be measured against, ranked on
   a defensible basis, and where does EOV genuinely sit against them?
5. **Customers** — Who buys, how do they buy, what do they actually decide on, and what
   makes them switch?
6. **Market need** — Where are incumbents failing clients, and which of those gaps can
   EOV credibly own?
7. **Proof standard** — What does a world-class credentials deck in this category contain,
   and what evidence must EOV assemble to meet that bar?

## 2. Market definition & taxonomy (do this first — it's the step most often skipped)

"Integrated marketing agency" is a self-applied label with no standard definition. Before
we can rank anyone, we have to bound the category. Deliverable: a one-page market map.

### 2.1 Define integration on three axes
An agency is only meaningfully "integrated" if it can answer yes across these. We score
every competitor on the same axes.

- **Disciplinary breadth** — strategy · brand/creative · content & production · media
  planning & buying · performance/digital · social & influencer · CRM/martech · data &
  analytics · PR/comms · experiential & events · web/product build.
- **Structural integration** — is it one P&L and one delivery team, or separately-run units
  cross-selling? (The single most common false claim in this category.)
- **Accountability depth** — do they take responsibility for business outcomes, or only
  for deliverables? Retainer-and-outcome vs project-and-output.

### 2.2 Classify the supply side into archetypes
Every competitor gets exactly one archetype. This is what makes a tiered ranking possible.

| Archetype | Description | MENA examples to verify |
|---|---|---|
| A. Holding-company network arm | Regional office of a global group | Publicis Groupe MEA, WPP/VML MENA, Omnicom MENA, Dentsu MENA, Havas Middle East |
| B. Regional network / affiliate | MENA-owned or MENA-run network | Horizon Holdings (FP7 McCann), Impact BBDO, TBWA\Raad, Memac Ogilvy, Leo Burnett MEA |
| C. Integrated independent (mid) | Independent, multi-discipline, 50–250 staff | Grow Combine, Create Media Group, Socialize/Tonic, Netizency, Serviceplan ME |
| D. Digital-native / performance shop | Digital-first, often SEO/paid-led | Digital Gravity, Nexa, Traffic Digital, Element8, Bird, Prakis |
| E. Government/public-sector specialist | Public-sector-weighted, often bilingual, often tech-adjacent | **EOV's current home** — to be mapped |
| F. Consultancy-owned | Consulting firms with creative arms | Accenture Song ME, Deloitte Digital ME, PwC Experience Centre |
| G. Production & content studio | Execution-led, film/CGI/events | To be mapped |
| H. AI-native entrant | Post-2023, AI-leveraged delivery model | To be mapped |

### 2.3 Map substitutes and new entrants (these compress pricing, not just share)
- **In-housing** — UAE government entities and large private clients building internal
  creative/media teams. The single biggest structural threat to EOV's core revenue.
- **Consultancies** moving down from transformation into brand and campaign.
- **Freelance collectives / unbundled talent marketplaces.**
- **Platform-direct** — Meta/Google/TikTok creative and media services sold direct to advertiser.
- **AI tooling replacing production line-items** — the specific risk to a production-rooted P&L.
- **Systems integrators** winning "digital identity / digital transformation" work that
  EOV currently claims.

### 2.4 Decide EOV's served market
Output of this section is a single explicit statement: *the market EOV is entering is
[X disciplines] for [Y buyer types] in [Z geography] at [budget band]*. Everything
downstream is scoped to that statement. Without it, the top 15 is arbitrary.

## 3. Workstream A — Market architecture, size, growth & depth

**Question:** How big is the prize, how fast is it moving, and how deep is it?

### A1. Sizing (three nested layers, built bottom-up *and* top-down, then reconciled)
- **TAM** — total UAE/GCC marketing services expenditure (agency gross income, not billings —
  the distinction matters enormously and is routinely conflated).
- **SAM** — the disciplines and buyer types EOV can credibly serve per §2.4.
- **SOM** — realistically winnable in 24–36 months given EOV's headcount, capacity and roster.

Method: top-down from published adspend and agency-income data; bottom-up from
(number of qualified buyers × average annual agency spend × win rate). Reconcile the two
and report the gap honestly rather than picking the flattering number.

### A2. Growth and the direction of spend
Not just "is it growing" but **where the money is migrating**: brand vs performance,
retail media, influencer/creator, in-house vs agency, content volume economics, AI's
effect on production fees, and the UAE/Saudi public-sector spend cycle (Vision 2030,
UAE Centennial 2071, Expo/major-event legacy budgets).

### A3. Market depth (the part usually missed)
Depth = how much revenue a single won relationship is worth over time.
- Number of qualified buying organisations in the served market.
- Average annual spend per buyer, by segment and by tier.
- Purchase frequency: one-off project vs annual retainer vs multi-year framework.
- Contract length and renewal rates; typical agency tenure in MENA.
- Client lifetime value and the realistic ceiling on account growth.
- **Government procurement specifics:** tender/framework panels, pre-qualification
  requirements, Emiratisation and local-content rules, payment terms and their cashflow
  consequences. This is decisive for EOV and generic agency research will miss it entirely.

### A4. PESTLE, scoped tightly
Only factors that change a decision: UAE/GCC economic outlook and adspend correlation;
government spending cycles; media regulation and content rules; data/privacy regime;
AI regulation; Arabic-language and cultural localisation requirements; free-zone vs
mainland licensing and its effect on who can bid for government work; talent visas and
salary inflation.

**Output:** Market architecture note — map, sizing model with stated assumptions, growth
vectors, depth table, PESTLE shortlist.

## 4. Workstream B — Competitive landscape & the Top 15 ranking

**Question:** Who are the top 15, on what defensible basis, and where does EOV sit?

### 4.1 Longlist construction (target 60–80 agencies, then cut)
Built from: MENA award show entrant/winner lists · regional trade press agency rankings ·
new-business league tables · government tender award records where public · directory
platforms · digital-footprint analysis · LinkedIn company data · client-side roster
disclosures. Sources named in §4.4.

### 4.2 Tiering (non-negotiable — a single flat list would be misleading)
The top 15 will be delivered as **three ranked tiers of five**, so the list is decision-useful:

- **Tier 1 — Category setters (5).** The agencies defining standards, winning the awards and
  the flagship government/enterprise accounts. EOV benchmarks its *craft and proof standard*
  against these. It does not pitch against them today.
- **Tier 2 — Direct competitive set (5).** Independent and mid-market integrated agencies
  EOV will meet in the same pitches and on the same tender panels. **This is the tier that
  matters most and where research depth is concentrated.**
- **Tier 3 — Insurgents and flankers (5).** Digital-natives, AI-native entrants and
  specialists compressing price and redefining buyer expectations from below.

### 4.3 Ranking scorecard (weighted, transparent, applied identically to all)
Each agency scored 1–5 per criterion; weighted total drives rank. The scorecard is
published with the ranking so the client can challenge the weights.

| # | Criterion | Weight | What we measure |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | Integration depth | 20% | Score on the three axes in §2.1 — genuine single-P&L breadth, not claimed |
| 2 | Scale & financial strength | 15% | Revenue/gross income, headcount, offices, ownership backing |
| 3 | Client roster quality & retention | 15% | Blue-chip and government logos, account tenure, AOR vs project |
| 4 | Creative & effectiveness proof | 15% | Dubai Lynx, Cannes, Effie MENA, WARC, MENA Effies — weighted to *effectiveness* over craft |
| 5 | Public-sector credibility | 10% | Government roster, framework/panel status, Arabic capability, security/compliance posture — **EOV-specific criterion** |
| 6 | Growth-vector capability | 10% | Data/martech, commerce, AI-in-delivery, in-housing enablement |
| 7 | Market presence & share of voice | 10% | Digital footprint, search visibility, PR share, event presence |
| 8 | Momentum | 5% | New-business wins, growth rate, senior hires, M&A, last 24 months |

### 4.4 Per-agency intelligence profile (identical template for all 15)
Positioning statement in their own words · archetype · ownership · size · offices ·
service list vs §2.1 axes · named clients and sectors · sector skew · pricing/commercial
model where discoverable · flagship work · awards · leadership team · stated
differentiator · **observed strengths** · **observed weaknesses and vulnerabilities** ·
likely forward strategy · **and specifically: how EOV would beat them in a pitch, and
how they would beat EOV.** That last field is the one that actually feeds the deck.

### 4.5 Source stack
- **Rankings & trade:** Campaign Middle East, ArabAd, Ad Age Agency Report, Adweek,
  WARC Rankings, Gulf Marketing Review.
- **New business & billings:** COMvergence, R3 league tables, MediaSense.
- **Awards:** Dubai Lynx, Cannes Lions Global Creativity Report, Effie MENA, MENA Digital Awards.
- **Analyst:** Forrester Wave / Gartner on global and regional marketing agencies.
- **Financial:** holding-company annual reports and segment disclosures for MEA.
- **Digital footprint:** Semrush (available in this environment) for traffic, keyword,
  backlink and share-of-search benchmarking across the full longlist — a quantitative,
  directly comparable dataset most competitor decks lack.
- **Directories (mid-market discovery only, treated as low-trust):** Clutch, Sortlist,
  DesignRush, The Manifest, Edvido.
- **Primary-adjacent:** LinkedIn headcount trends and hiring patterns; job postings as a
  capability signal; Glassdoor/Bayt for delivery-model and churn signals.

**Output:** Ranked top 15 in three tiers · scorecard with all scores visible ·
15 intelligence profiles · positioning map (2 axes, chosen from the data) ·
EOV's honest placement on the same scorecard.

## 5. Workstream C — Customers, buying behaviour & segmentation

**Question:** Who buys, how, and on what basis? This is the workstream most agency
research skips, and it is the one that makes a credentials deck persuasive rather than decorative.

### C1. Segment the demand side (needs-based, not just firmographic)
Candidate segments to test: UAE federal government entities · Dubai/Abu Dhabi
government and semi-government · GREs and statutory authorities · large private
enterprise · regional family conglomerates · mid-market private · startups/scale-ups ·
multinationals' regional marketing hubs.

Test every segment against the five screens: **reachable · distinct · definable ·
identifiable · large enough to be profitable.** Segments failing a screen are named and dropped.

### C2. Map the buying unit
For each priority segment: economic buyer · technical/evaluating buyer · user buyer ·
**procurement's role and veto power** · internal champion · blocker. Government
procurement behaves nothing like private-sector CMO buying and must be modelled separately.

### C3. Anatomy of the purchase
- Trigger events (new CMO, budget cycle, roster review, campaign failure, restructure,
  mandate change).
- Route to market: RFP/tender · framework panel · credentials meeting · referral ·
  inbound · relationship.
- Process stages and typical duration; who is in the room at each stage.
- **What is actually asked for at the credentials stage vs the pitch stage** — these are
  different documents, and knowing the difference determines what we build.
- Evaluation and scoring criteria (for government tenders these are frequently published —
  a rare source of literal, weighted buying criteria).
- Deal shape: retainer vs project vs FTE-based vs performance-linked; typical values and terms.

### C4. Decision criteria hierarchy
Rank what buyers say matters against what evidence suggests actually decides it — sector
experience, senior-team access, price, speed, cultural/language fit, technology, case
proof, chemistry, risk reduction. Then identify which criteria are **table stakes** and
which are genuine **differentiators**, because only the latter belong in the deck.

### C5. Switching and retention
Why clients leave; average agency tenure in MENA; what makes a relationship stick;
what a "safe choice" looks like to a risk-averse government buyer.

**Output:** Segment definitions with screen results · buying-unit maps · purchase-process
map · ranked decision-criteria table (table stakes vs differentiators) · switching triggers.

## 6. Workstream D — Market need, whitespace & threat map

**Question:** Where do incumbents fail, and which gap can EOV credibly own?

### D1. Systematic complaint mining
Evidence, not assertion. Sources: ANA studies on agency compensation, transparency and
in-housing · Forrester/Gartner CMO surveys · trade-press coverage of account losses and
review outcomes · agency review-site and employer-review commentary · published
tender debriefs · LinkedIn practitioner discourse.

Recurring failure patterns to test and evidence: senior-talent bait-and-switch · siloed
delivery sold as integrated · opaque pricing · slow turnaround · no outcome
accountability · weak Arabic/cultural fluency · junior teams on senior fees ·
inability to handle government compliance and process.

### D2. Whitespace identification
Cross the failure map against the competitive scorecard to find positions that are
**underserved, defensible, and credible for EOV specifically** — tested against EOV's
real assets (20-year longevity, government track record, technology/digital-identity
capability, bilingual delivery, independence and senior-team access, royal recognition).
A whitespace EOV cannot prove is not a whitespace; it is a liability.

### D3. Threat map
Rank the substitutes from §2.3 by likelihood × impact on EOV's revenue over 36 months.
Name the ones that must be neutralised in the deck's narrative rather than ignored.

**Output:** Evidenced pain-point register · whitespace options with credibility ratings ·
ranked threat map · preliminary SWOT for EOV built entirely on cited evidence.

## 7. Workstream E — Credentials benchmark (competitive communications audit)

**Question:** What does world-class look like in this specific document type, and what
proof must EOV assemble to clear that bar?

- Deconstruct 12–15 credentials/profile decks from Tier 1 and Tier 2 agencies: structure,
  section order, length, opening move, how capability is evidenced, how case studies are
  built, how results are stated, how the team is presented, how the close works.
- Identify the **proof architecture** that recurs in the strongest ones: capability →
  named client → specific challenge → what we did → quantified outcome → named accountable person.
- Catalogue the standard claims (so EOV avoids them) and the rare, credible ones (so EOV can use them).
- Establish visual and production standards for the category.
- **Run an evidence audit against EOV**: for each proof point the benchmark demands, does
  EOV have it, could it get it, or must the claim be dropped? This produces a concrete
  evidence-collection task list for the client — case-study metrics, client permissions
  and logo rights, testimonials, team bios, certifications, tender pre-qualifications.

**Output:** Benchmark analysis · proof-architecture spec · EOV evidence-gap register with
a collection checklist.

## 8. Workstream F — Synthesis

Converges the above into the bridge between research and build. **Not started until
A–E are complete and reviewed.**

Market conclusions · EOV's honest competitive position · recommended target segment(s) ·
recommended positioning territory with the evidence supporting it · USP worked
feature → benefit → importance → standard-or-different · the six-line positioning
statement · messaging hierarchy by segment · objection-handling map · and the
**creative brief for the deck**: audience, purpose, single proposition, required proof,
structure, tone, mandatories, success measure.

---

## 9. Evidence standard (applies to everything above)

1. **No invented figures.** No market size, growth rate, revenue, headcount or client
   count without a source. Where a number is needed and unavailable, it is marked
   `[SOURCE NEEDED]` with a note on what would fill it.
2. **Every material claim carries a source and a date.** Agency data ages fast.
3. **Confidence rating on every finding** — High (primary or multiple independent
   secondary) / Medium (single credible secondary) / Low (inference, directory data,
   self-reported).
4. **Self-reported agency claims are labelled as claims,** never restated as fact.
   Directory listings (Clutch, DesignRush, etc.) are discovery tools, not evidence.
5. **Contradictions are surfaced, not smoothed.** Where sources conflict, both are shown.
6. **Assumptions are stated explicitly and separately** from findings.

### Open items required from the client before Phase 1
1. **Verified EOV baseline** — legal name, current headcount and structure, revenue band,
   real service list (in-house vs subcontracted), Arabic delivery capability, licensing
   (free-zone vs mainland) and any government pre-qualifications held.
2. **Client roster and case evidence** — named clients, permissions to cite, retained vs
   project, tenure, and any quantified outcomes on record.
3. **Commercial model** — how EOV prices and what a typical engagement is worth.
4. **Ambition** — which segments and budget bands EOV wants to win, and any it will not serve.
5. **Site content** — `eov.ae` is egress-blocked from this environment; content will need
   to be supplied directly.

### Explicitly out of scope at this stage
Deck design, narrative, copy, case-study writing, visual system, or any build. None of it
starts until §8 is signed off.
