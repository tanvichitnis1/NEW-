# EOV — Workstream C: Demand-Side Findings (First Pass)

Credentials-stage buying behaviour, switching drivers, and what a first introduction
has to do. Promoted to a core workstream by the client's clarification that the deck is
a **universal introduction to any prospective client**, not primarily a tender document.

Evidence standard per design §9. Confidence: **H** / **M** / **L**.

> **Source-quality warning.** Much of the published material on "why clients leave
> agencies" originates from agencies selling retention services, and the percentages
> circulate without traceable methodology. Figures below are labelled accordingly.
> Industry-body research (ANA, 4As) is treated as materially more reliable than agency
> blog content, and the two disagree — see the contradiction at §3.

## 1. What buyers want from a first introduction

The most useful finding is also the most inconvenient one for a traditional credentials deck.

| Finding | Conf. | Source |
|---|:--:|---|
| A credentials deck must answer three questions: **who are you, what do you do, why should we trust you** | M | Manifest, Pitchsite |
| High-value clients are not looking for more information — they are looking for **confidence and proof they won't become another expensive mistake** | M | Ink Narrates |
| Case studies are the engine; strongest form is **challenge → solution → outcome with specific, verifiable metrics** | M | Multiple |
| Clients want **the journey behind the work**, not only polished outcomes — how budget was managed, what went wrong, where strategy pivoted | M | Manifest |
| **Less is more.** Sharp relevant highlights beat comprehensive capability coverage | M | Multiple |
| Credentials must be **tailored to the audience's sector and challenges**, not a fixed recital | M | Multiple |
| **Put faces to the work** — name the individuals, not just the agency | M | Multiple |
| A first/chemistry meeting is **not the venue for an exhaustive deck or a slideshow of past work** — it is a discovery conversation to test alignment | M | TrinityP3, Manifest |
| Clients still expect credentials in the room and expect the agency to prove it is up to the job | M | TrinityP3 |
| **Poor knowledge of your own case studies is a red flag** that loses meetings | M | TrinityP3 |
| Clients dislike over-reliance on "nitty gritty facts and figures" — and getting one wrong **turns the meeting sour quickly** | M | TrinityP3 |
| Interpersonal chemistry is among the most important selection criteria | M | TrinityP3 |
| CMOs frequently defer the decision to **the team who will work with the agency daily** — so all client attendees must be engaged equally, not just the senior one | M | TrinityP3 |
| Traditional PowerPoint is not always the best format; interactive microsites, video reels and online portfolios are credible alternatives | M | Multiple |

### What these findings tell us about the market

Recorded as market intelligence. Nothing here specifies how anything gets built —
that is a Stage 2 question and out of scope.

1. **Buyers in this category are information-saturated and confidence-starved.**
   Every source converges on it. The scarce commodity in agency selection is not
   capability information, it is trust and risk reduction.
2. **An unresolved tension exists in the buying process itself.** Buyers want enough
   substance to pre-qualify an agency before meeting it, yet the first meeting is
   explicitly not the venue for a full presentation. Worth understanding properly,
   because it means the introduction and the meeting are doing two different jobs
   for the buyer.
3. **Verifiability is the operative standard for case evidence** — not richness,
   not volume. Unquantified project descriptions do not register as proof.
4. **A wrong or undefendable figure is worse than no figure.** The downside is
   asymmetric: it loses the meeting.
5. **Buyers expect named individuals.** The persistent industry complaint behind
   this is senior-talent bait-and-switch — the people who present are not the people
   who deliver. Structurally, small independents can answer this and network agencies
   cannot, which is worth testing against EOV's competitive set in Workstream B.

## 2. Why clients leave — the failure map

Ranked drivers of agency switching. **These specific percentages come from a
retention-services provider with no published methodology and are rated Low
confidence.** They are directionally consistent with better-sourced material and are
used here to *generate hypotheses*, not as quotable facts.

| Driver | Reported | Conf. |
|---|:--:|:--:|
| Lack of proactive strategic guidance — clients want partners who challenge their thinking, not order-takers | 68% | L |
| Poor communication and transparency — having to chase for updates erodes trust | 57% | L |
| Inability to demonstrate value — work not connected to business outcomes leadership cares about | 53% | L |
| Dissatisfaction with delivery | 48% | L |
| Clients expecting to switch agency partners within six months | 40% | L |

Better-sourced adjacent findings:

| Finding | Conf. | Source |
|---|:--:|---|
| **90% of clients prioritise value over cost reduction** | M | 4As / ANA joint study |
| **31% rated trust with their media agency low or very low** | M | ANA |
| Average agency review runs **longer than three months**; at least two months even with an incumbent involved | M | ANA / 4As |
| Media rebates remain a live client-agency trust issue | M | ANA |

**Reading across these:** the three top reported failure drivers are not capability
failures. They are **failures of proactivity, transparency and outcome accountability** —
all of them relationship and operating-model problems, not skills gaps. This is the
central strategic finding of the workstream so far, and it points somewhere very
specific for EOV: the market's dissatisfaction is concentrated exactly where a small,
senior, independent agency is structurally advantaged and a large network is
structurally weak.

That is a candidate whitespace. It is **not yet a proven one** — it requires EOV to
demonstrate it operationally, and it is the most commonly *claimed* territory by every
small agency in the world. Claiming it is worthless; proving it is the whole game.
Carried to Workstream D.

## 3. Contradiction — surfaced, not smoothed

Two credible-sounding claims are in direct conflict:

- **"40% of clients will switch agency partners in the next six months."** (Low conf.,
  retention-services provider.)
- **"Client-agency relationship tenure has doubled since 2016."** (Higher conf.,
  ANA and 4As joint report, April 2025.)

These cannot both describe the same market. The ANA/4As figure is the better-sourced
and is from an industry body with no product to sell against churn. **Resolution
required before either is used.** Probable explanation: the 40% measures stated
intent in a self-selected survey sample; the ANA figure measures actual observed tenure.
If the ANA finding holds, it changes EOV's new-business strategy materially — longer
tenures mean **fewer contestable accounts, higher switching costs, and a much higher
premium on being on the roster when a review does happen.** That would make the
introduction deck's job partly about *presence and readiness* rather than persuasion at
a single moment.

Flagged as a priority item for the full Workstream C pass.

## 4. Archetype E build-out — government specialists

The gap identified in `03-longlist-seed.md` is beginning to fill. Two confirmed direct
competitors, both narrower than EOV in different ways:

**Bird Marketing** (Dubai Silicon Oasis) — runs a **dedicated government and
public-sector practice**, positioned on public-sector website accessibility, efficiency
and navigability, with SEO optimisation specific to government. Awards are
directory-derived (Clutch, GoodFirms) rather than industry-juried. Positions as
full-service digital but the evidenced depth is search and paid media.
*Assessment: narrower than EOV on integration; sharper than EOV on public-sector
digital positioning and on published thought leadership.* (Conf. M)

**CRÉO** (creoglobal.co) — explicitly positions across **B2G, B2G2C, B2B and B2C**,
spanning marketing, communications, data and advertising, with a PR and events practice.
The B2G2C framing is unusually sophisticated and is the closest stated positioning to
EOV's territory found so far. *Assessment: likely EOV's most direct positioning
competitor. Priority for a full §4.4 profile.* (Conf. M)

**Still missing.** Neither of these is an integrated agency of EOV's type, and the
archetype remains under-mapped. The reason is structural and worth stating plainly:
public-sector agency work **rarely wins creative awards and rarely appears in trade
press**, so it is close to invisible to the research methods that surface Archetypes A–D.
The remaining build-out must come from **government tender award records, supplier
registries and pre-qualified vendor lists** rather than media. That is slower, and it is
where the genuinely useful competitive intelligence for EOV will be found.

## 5. Escalated: the citable-evidence question

Three findings above converge on the same constraint, which makes it a research
question rather than a later production detail:

- Case evidence must carry **specific, verifiable metrics**
- Buyers want the **journey**, including what went wrong
- A wrong or undefendable figure **loses the meeting**

For a government-weighted roster this is genuinely hard — outcomes are often
confidential and client names may not be citable at all.

**Why it belongs in the research phase:** whether EOV can cite named public-sector
clients and quantified outcomes determines what kind of proof is even available to it,
which in turn determines which market positions are open to EOV and which are closed.
It is an input to the whitespace analysis in Workstream D, not a production question.
Added to the client-input list.
